  1. The question. Does the order of records change how many get an ID, and which?
    More on the question

    Each order asks one of two questions. Speed: does it change how many IDs an hour of work gives? Value: does it change which gaps the IDs fill, for example places where few species are recorded? Newest first, close to what iNaturalist shows today, is the control for both. More in the README.

  2. The lists. Each record goes to one list at random, so on average every list holds the same mix of taxon groups and observers. Only the order is different:
    • Newest first. The control, close to what iNaturalist shows today.
    • Data-poor places first. Records from areas with few or old records.
    • Look-alike photos together. Similar photos come in groups.
    • Unfamiliar photos first. Photos least like any Research Grade photo.
    More on the lists

    This build holds 10,000 British Columbia records that needed an ID and had a photo when pulled on 2026-09-11 (inat.py).

    This build shuffled the records once, with a private seed. The shuffle runs inside each set of records that share a taxon group and an observer group (3 observer groups from a hash of the user number, and one more for records with no user) and deals the records to the lists in turn, so inside each set the lists differ in size by one record at most.

    Then each list sorts its records, one taxon group at a time, and cuts them into batches of up to 120 records. This build serves the first 20 batches of each list in each group.

    • Newest first sorts by the time the record was added to iNaturalist, newest first.
    • Data-poor places first sorts by the where-to-blitz map score of the nearest 25 km map cell for the record's taxon group, highest first. The score is the map's "Species discovery" setting: its discovery measure plus 0.6 times its record-age measure, cut to 0 to 1. A record more than 20 km from a cell centre has no score and goes last. A taxon group without its own map uses the map for all groups.
    • Look-alike photos together uses an image model (BioCLIP 2.5) that turns each photo into numbers, so photos that look alike sit close together. It picks start photos spread across all the photos and grows a group of about one batch around each, adding the free photo closest to the group centre. Groups with the highest mean map score come first. Batches are cut from this order, so a batch can end one group and start the next.
    • Unfamiliar photos first compares each photo, with the same image model, with every Research Grade photo of its taxon group in the reference pull. Records whose closest verified photo is least alike come first.

    Code: assign.py, arms.py, cells.py, batches.py.

    Each batch is one iNaturalist Identify link with the record numbers and the BC place. Identify hides records that no longer need an ID and records you already reviewed (checked in a browser on 2026-09-11). The design borrows the within-person comparison of interleaved search tests (Chapelle et al. 2012).

    Code: page_rotation.py (the nextBatch function).

  4. The count. After the blitz, we count the records each participant gave a species-level ID on each list and compare each person with themself. A fast identifier puts the same effort into every list.
    More on the count

    The analysis will be fixed before the blitz. The current plan, in the draft protocol:

    • Who counts. Participants are the identifiers who join the blitz's iNaturalist project.
    • What counts. A participant's ID at species level or below (species, subspecies, variety, form or hybrid) on a record this page served, made between the blitz start and the cut-off date. A record counts once per person.
    • Weights. Data-poor places first is judged on a weighted count: each record counts its map score, from 0 to 1, in place of 1, on newest first too. A record with no score counts 0.
    • The test. For each participant, their count on a list minus their count on newest first. The test adds these differences, then flips the sign of each person's difference at random and adds again. People with no difference drop out. With 12 or fewer people left it tries every pattern, else 10,000 random ones. If few flipped sums are as far from zero as the real one, the difference is not chance. Two-sided at 5 percent, adjusted (Holm) when more than one list is compared with the control.
    • Also reported. A sign test on how many participants did better on each list, and, 30 days after the blitz ends, the share of served records that reached species level.
    • Checks. The same test on the days before the blitz, before participants were sent the page, should find nothing. Each participant's reviewed records should split about evenly over the lists.

    Code: readback.py, analysis.py; power.py simulates both page designs to estimate how many participants the test needs.

  5. Nothing else changes. You identify in iNaturalist as usual. Your IDs carry your name, count toward Research Grade and go to GBIF.
    More on what stays the same

    The page holds only list letters and Identify links. Which letter is which list is kept private and, by the draft protocol, opened after the read-back. The lists are unlabelled rather than blind: you can sometimes tell a list by its batches. The test does not depend on you not knowing, because it compares you with yourself.

    Identify shows each batch newest first and you can skip freely, so a batch sets which records you meet together, not the order you identify them in. A result holds for this region and these identifiers.

    In the blitz, each daily build runs again from its saved inputs, and the job stops unless the rerun serves exactly the batches it logged (replay.py).
